Unit 1.1  ·  Reconstruction

Plan for Reconstruction

The war is over. Now comes the harder question.

Before you plan

It is the spring of 1865. The Civil War has just ended. Over 600,000 people are dead. The South is in ruins. And 4 million people who were enslaved are now legally free with no land, no money, few legal protections, and no guarantee that their freedom will last. Someone has to decide what happens next. What does freedom actually mean? What happens to the people who ran the Confederacy? Who rebuilds the South, and how? Who gets a say?

These are not abstract questions. The decisions made in the next few years will determine what kind of country this is. Real people's lives hang on what gets decided.

Here are five of them.

Five people who have everything at stake in what happens next

The Freedman
Georgia, 1865

He is 48. He has worked this land his entire life without pay, without choice, without any legal right to his own family. The war ended three months ago and he is still on the same plantation, because there is nowhere else to go and nothing to go with.

WantsLand. Enough to feed his family and never answer to a white man's orders again.
FearsThat freedom on paper means nothing if he ends up in the same field doing the same work with no real way out.
The Freed Woman
South Carolina, 1865

She is 31. Her husband was sold to a plantation in Alabama seven years ago. She has not heard from him since. She has a daughter who is two years old. She is free now, legally, but she does not know where her husband is or whether he is alive.

WantsTo find her husband. To keep her daughter. To never have her family separated by someone else's decision again.
FearsThat without land, money, or legal standing, she is free in name only and just as vulnerable as before.
The Black Union Veteran
Virginia, 1865

He is 26. He enlisted in 1863, fought in four major engagements, watched men die beside him. He wore the uniform of the United States Army and fought for this country. He is now riding home through states that do not consider him a citizen.

WantsThe rights he earned. The vote. Equal standing under the law. Recognition that his service meant something.
FearsThat the country he fought for will thank him and then put him back exactly where he started.
The Plantation Owner
Mississippi, 1865

He is 54. He enslaved over 60 people on this land. His oldest son died at Vicksburg. The land is still there but he has no one to work it and no money to pay anyone. He was on the losing side of a war but he does not feel like he was wrong.

WantsHis land back, his debts forgiven, and things returned as close to before as possible.
FearsLosing his property, his status, and any political voice in what the South becomes.
The Southern Unionist
Tennessee, 1865

He is 44, a white farmer who never owned enslaved people and never supported secession. He kept his head down during the war. He is exhausted and does not think Black Americans should be enslaved, but he is not sure what comes next and is not eager to find out.

WantsPeace. Stability. To rebuild his farm without more upheaval or federal soldiers telling him what to do.
FearsMore conflict, and being lumped in with Confederates he never supported.

Three plans that were actually proposed

What the people in power were considering

Three different men had three very different ideas about what Reconstruction should look like. Read them. Think about which one comes closest to what your group proposed, and what each one leaves out.

Abraham Lincoln
The 10 Percent Plan
  • A Southern state could rejoin when 10% of its voters swore loyalty to the Union
  • Confederate leaders would be pardoned if they swore loyalty
  • States had to accept the end of slavery
  • No guaranteed rights or land for formerly enslaved people
  • Goal: reunite the country quickly and with as little conflict as possible
What does this plan get right? What does it leave out for the people you just read about?
Andrew Johnson
Presidential Reconstruction
  • Even more lenient than Lincoln's plan
  • Most Confederates pardoned quickly, including military officers
  • Southern states could write their own new constitutions
  • No requirement to give Black Americans any rights
  • Southern states immediately passed Black Codes to control freed people
Johnson was from Tennessee. Look back at the Southern Unionist. Does Johnson's plan reflect what that character wanted?
Thaddeus Stevens
Congressional Reconstruction
  • Southern states treated as conquered territory, not equal partners
  • Federal troops stationed throughout the South to enforce order
  • 14th Amendment: citizenship and equal protection for Black Americans
  • 15th Amendment: right to vote regardless of race
  • Stevens personally pushed for land redistribution, but it never passed
This is the plan that came closest to what most groups propose. So why did it fail?
What actually happened

The Reckoning

Congressional Reconstruction passed. The amendments were ratified. Federal troops went south. And then, one by one, it came apart. Read what happened and think about whether your plan would have changed anything.

April 1873

The Colfax Massacre

On Easter Sunday in Colfax, Louisiana, a white supremacist mob attacked a group of Black men defending the local courthouse after a disputed election. Over 150 Black men were killed, many of them after they had already surrendered. It was one of the deadliest acts of racial violence in American history.

Three white men were convicted of federal crimes. The Supreme Court threw out the convictions, ruling the federal government could not prosecute private individuals for violating civil rights. The law existed. No one enforced it.

Your group talked about how to protect the rights of Black Americans in the South. Would your plan have prevented this? What would it have actually taken to stop it?
1875

The Mississippi Plan

White Democrats in Mississippi organized a campaign of targeted violence and intimidation to keep Black voters from the polls. They showed up armed at Republican meetings. They threatened anyone who tried to register. They murdered Black political leaders. It worked. Democrats swept the state elections and took back control of Mississippi's government.

The federal government watched and did almost nothing. President Grant declined to send troops. Within a few years the same strategy had spread across the South.

The 14th and 15th Amendments were already law. The right to vote was already protected on paper. What does it mean when the law exists but no one enforces it? What would enforcement have actually required?
1877

The Compromise of 1877

The presidential election of 1876 was disputed. After months of backroom negotiations, Republican Rutherford Hayes was named president in exchange for one thing: the federal government would pull all remaining troops out of the South. Reconstruction was over. The last people protecting Black political and civil rights left.

Within years, every Southern state had passed laws stripping Black Americans of the vote, access to education, economic mobility, and basic legal protection. It would take nearly 90 more years before those rights were formally restored.

Your group talked about who should lead the South and how to rebuild it. What would it have taken to make sure the people who lost the war could not just wait out Reconstruction and take back power?
