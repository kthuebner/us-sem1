Part 1  ·  March 25, 1931

Riding the Rails

The Great Depression is two years old. Millions of Americans have no job and no money for a ticket, so they do something illegal but common: they hop onto freight trains and ride from town to town looking for work.

On March 25, 1931, a freight train pulls out of Chattanooga, Tennessee, heading into Alabama. Riding on it are nine young Black men and teenagers. Most of them do not know each other. The youngest is twelve or thirteen. The oldest is nineteen or twenty.

A fight breaks out between some of the Black teens and a group of white teens riding the same train. The white teens lose and get pushed off. They walk back to the last station and report the fight. The stationmaster phones ahead.

At the tiny town of Paint Rock, Alabama, a posse of armed white men stops the train and pulls off every Black rider they can find. Two young white women climb out of a boxcar too: Victoria Price, about twenty one, and Ruby Bates, seventeen. Within minutes, they say the nine raped them.

Part 2

Why That Accusation Was So Dangerous

You need to understand what that accusation meant in the South in 1931. This is the Jim Crow era. A white woman accusing a Black man of rape was treated as proof. Black men accused of it were often lynched, which means killed by a mob without any trial at all.

The nine were locked in the jail in Scottsboro, the county seat, which is how they got their name. That night a mob gathered outside the jail. The governor of Alabama sent the National Guard to keep the mob from breaking in. The nine would get a trial. The question was whether it would be anything more than a lynching with paperwork.

So why did they lie?

Price and Bates were poor mill workers from Huntsville who had crossed a state line on a freight train with men. They could have been arrested for vagrancy, which means wandering with no job or home, or under a federal law called the Mann Act, which made it a crime to cross state lines for "immoral purposes." Accusing someone else moved the danger off of them and onto nine strangers. The video explains this starting around the three minute mark.

Part 3

What the Evidence Actually Showed

About ninety minutes after the train stopped, a local doctor, Dr. R. R. Bridges, examined both women. He found no injuries that fit their story. He described them as calm, not upset. If two women had just been attacked by nine people on a moving train, that is not what a doctor would find.

Later, when the defense team finally rebuilt the train car by car, they showed that many of the nine were not even in the same car as the women. Some of them said they never saw the women at all until Paint Rock.

None of this stopped what happened next.

Part 4  ·  April 1931

Twelve Days to Trial

The trials started just twelve days after the arrests. Thousands of white people crowded into Scottsboro to watch. Every juror was white.

The nine were given two lawyers on the morning the trial began. One was Stephen Roddy, a real estate lawyer from Tennessee who admitted he had not prepared and did not know Alabama law. The video says he was drunk. The other was Milo Moody, sixty nine years old, who had not defended a case in decades. Neither lawyer talked to the defendants in any real way before the trial started.

The court ran four trials in about four days. Eight of the nine were convicted and sentenced to death in the electric chair.

The ninth, Roy Wright, was only twelve or thirteen. The prosecutor asked for life in prison because of his age. Eleven jurors voted for death anyway. One held out, so the judge declared a mistrial, which means the trial ended with no verdict. Roy Wright stayed in jail.

Who took the case

Why Would the Communist Party Get Involved?

Communism is the idea that working people, not wealthy owners, should control a country's farms, factories, and wealth. In the Great Depression, with banks failing and millions out of work, a lot of Americans started listening to that idea. The Communist Party USA was small, but it was loud.

The Communist Party argued that racism was a tool the rich used to keep Black and white workers fighting each other instead of fighting their bosses. So, unlike almost every other white organization in America at the time, it made equal rights for Black Americans a main goal. It organized Black sharecroppers in Alabama. It ran Black candidates for office. In 1932 its vice presidential candidate was James W. Ford, the first Black man in the twentieth century to run for vice president.

The party's legal team was called the International Labor Defense, or ILD. The ILD moved within days of the convictions. It sent lawyers, visited the boys' parents, and turned Scottsboro into a worldwide cause, with protests from Harlem to Europe.

The honest versionBoth of these things were true at once. The ILD genuinely saved the nine from the electric chair. It also wanted the case as a recruiting tool, to show Black Americans that the Communists fought for them when nobody else would. Critics said the ILD cared more about headlines than about winning. The families mostly did not care why. As Haywood Patterson's mother put it, the ILD was the only group that put up a fight.

One more detail: the ILD's most famous lawyer, Samuel Leibowitz, was not a Communist. He was a famous New York defense attorney who took the case for free because he was convinced the nine were innocent.

The group you would expect

Where Was the NAACP?

What the NAACP was

The National Association for the Advancement of Colored People, founded in 1909, was the biggest and most respected civil rights group in the country. It fought lynching, fought for voting rights, and used careful, well planned court cases to chip away at Jim Crow. It depended on donations and on its reputation.

Why it hesitated

  • The charge was rape. The NAACP had long avoided cases where Black men were accused of raping white women. If it defended nine young men who turned out to be guilty, its reputation could be ruined.
  • Its leaders were not sure yet whether the nine were innocent.
  • It was short on money, and it may have believed the nine already had real lawyers.

By the time the NAACP moved in, the ILD was already there. The two groups fought over the case. The NAACP offered one of the most famous lawyers in America, Clarence Darrow. The parents chose the ILD anyway, and Darrow withdrew. In 1935 the NAACP, the ILD, and others finally joined forces as the Scottsboro Defense Committee.

Scottsboro taught the NAACP a hard lesson about waiting. In 1940 it created the NAACP Legal Defense Fund, led by Thurgood Marshall, to take on these cases full time. Fourteen years later that team won Brown v. Board of Education.

Before the next part

Law 101

Almost everything that happened next happened in courtrooms. Here is what you need to know to follow it. Nothing more.

1
Trial

A judge and a jury hear the case. The jury decides guilty or not guilty. In Alabama in the 1930s, every jury was all white.

2
Appeal

The losing side asks a higher court to check whether the trial was done fairly. The higher court does not hold a new trial. It looks for mistakes.

3
State Supreme Court

The highest court in Alabama. For the Scottsboro Boys, it kept upholding the convictions.

4
U.S. Supreme Court

The highest court in the country. It can overturn a conviction if a state broke the Constitution. That sends the case back for a brand new trial.

The 14th Amendment

Added after the Civil War to protect formerly enslaved people. It says no state can take away a person's life, liberty, or property without due process, and no state can deny anyone equal protection of the law.

Due process

Fair treatment by the justice system at every step. Think of it as: the state has to play by the rules before it can punish you.

Equal protection

The law has to treat everyone the same. A state cannot run one justice system for white people and a different one for Black people.

Overturn and retrial

When a higher court throws out a conviction, the person is not automatically free. The state can put them on trial again. Alabama did this over and over.

The first big win

The Supreme Court Steps In

Powell v. Alabama

November 1932

The right to a real lawyer

What happened

The International Labor Defense appealed the first convictions all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court. The case is named after one defendant, Ozie Powell.

What the Court said

The Court said the nine had been denied due process. Being handed two unprepared lawyers the morning of a trial where your life is at stake is not a real defense. The convictions were thrown out.

Why it still matters

This was one of the first times the Supreme Court said the 14th Amendment requires a state to give a defendant a real lawyer. Years later, in Gideon v. Wainwright (1963), the Court built on it: anyone charged with a serious crime gets a lawyer, even if they cannot pay. It is part of the reason police today tell people under arrest that if they cannot afford a lawyer, one will be provided.

Part 5  ·  1932 to 1933

Ruby Bates Tells the Truth

Less than a year after the first trials, Ruby Bates wrote a letter to a boyfriend admitting the story was false.

Ruby Bates

Letter to her boyfriend, Earl Streetman, spelling as she wrote it

January 1932

those Negroes did not touch me.... i hope you will believe me the law dont.

Famous Trials archive, University of Missouri Kansas City School of Law

Notice

Read the last five words. She is not only saying the story was false. She is predicting what the courts will do with the truth.

Then she did something brave. In the spring of 1933, at Haywood Patterson's second trial in Decatur, Alabama, she walked into the courtroom as a surprise witness for the defense. She told the jury there had been no rape. She and Victoria Price had made it up to stay out of trouble.

It did not work. The prosecutor screamed at her and claimed she had been bought off by the Communists with new clothes. Another prosecutor told the jury not to let Jewish money from New York buy Alabama justice, a direct shot at Leibowitz. Victoria Price never took back her story. The all white jury found Patterson guilty and sentenced him to death, again.

The judge, James Horton, looked at the evidence and threw out the jury's verdict. It cost him his career. He lost the next election. Alabama gave the case to a different judge, and Patterson was convicted again.

Ruby Bates spent the next year giving speeches for the defense. In May 1933 she helped lead about four thousand people on a march to Washington, D.C., side by side with Haywood Patterson's mother.

The second big win

Back to the Supreme Court

Norris v. Alabama

April 1935

The right to a jury that includes people like you

What happened

Named after defendant Clarence Norris. His lawyer, Samuel Leibowitz, proved that no Black person had served on a jury in that county in living memory. Some Black names had been written onto the jury list after the fact, to make it look like they had been considered.

What the Court said

The Court said keeping Black citizens off juries because of their race violates equal protection. The convictions were thrown out again.

Why it still matters

It established that a jury picked by shutting out a whole race is not a fair jury. Later cases, like Batson v. Kentucky (1986), extended the idea: lawyers cannot strike jurors because of their race.

How Alabama got around it

Alabama found a way around Norris right away. For the next trial, officials put a handful of Black men in the jury pool. Then the prosecution used its right to reject jurors and struck every one of them. The jury was twelve white men again. Winning a right on paper and getting it in the courtroom were two different things.

Part 6  ·  1936 to 2013

How It Ended

In 1937, six years after the arrests, Alabama made a deal. It dropped every charge against four of the nine: Roy Wright, Eugene Williams, Olen Montgomery, and Willie Roberson. The state was quietly admitting they were innocent. But if four were innocent of a crime all nine were accused of together, so were the other five.

The other five stayed in prison anyway. Haywood Patterson, Clarence Norris, Andy Wright, and Charlie Weems were convicted of rape again. Ozie Powell's rape charge was dropped, but he got twenty years for attacking a deputy during a transfer.

In 1938, Governor Bibb Graves was ready to release them. He met with them first. They refused to say they were guilty of something they had not done, and they were angry with him. He changed his mind and left them in prison.

They got out one at a time, on parole, through the 1940s. The last one walked out in 1950. Haywood Patterson never got out legally. He escaped and made it to Michigan, and in 1950 Michigan's governor refused to send him back to Alabama.

In 1976, Governor George Wallace, famous for fighting integration, pardoned Clarence Norris, the last one still living under Alabama's sentence. In 2013, Alabama finally pardoned the last three who had never been cleared. All of them were dead by then.

Why It Still Matters

The nine did not get justice. But the fight over their case forced the Supreme Court to say two things out loud for the first time: a person on trial for their life gets a real lawyer, and a jury cannot be picked by shutting out an entire race.

It also taught a lot of people how to fight. Mass protest, national publicity, and patient court cases all got tested here. One young couple in Montgomery, Alabama, Raymond and Rosa Parks, spent years raising money for the Scottsboro defense. Twenty years later, Rosa Parks refused to give up her seat on a bus.

Whether that makes Scottsboro part of the civil rights movement is question ten. That one is yours.
